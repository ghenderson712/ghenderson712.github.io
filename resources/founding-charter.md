# Kin Vow

An imperfect union. A promise to each other.

Proposed founding charter, version 0.2 | October 31, 2026

Kin Vow is a working name pending rights review. This document proposes the movement's commitments. It has not been adopted as legal bylaws and does not establish an incorporated organization, tax status, or existing membership.

Kin Vow is a project in development. An adult pilot is being planned; no gatherings are scheduled. No host organization or official chapter is confirmed.

## Why we exist

America is an imperfect union, made of people whose lives are larger than the labels we give them. Kin Vow begins with a promise to recognize that humanity and act on it. We practice listening, useful cooperation, and repair while keeping the freedom to disagree and set boundaries. We begin close to home, with commitments small enough to keep.

Our mission is to build a national practice of treating one another with dignity. We want contempt and dehumanization to have less room in American civic life. A local pilot will help us learn whether our activities serve that purpose; it cannot establish a national effect.

Being kin means recognizing each person's worth and taking responsibility for how we treat them. People choose their relationships and boundaries. Kinship gives no claim to another person's time, trust, forgiveness, or closeness.

## The voluntary vow

> I will treat every person as if their inner world is as vast and valuable as my own.
> I will listen to understand, speak honestly, and leave room to be wrong.
> I will challenge harm without denying anyone's humanity.
> I will respect other people's boundaries and my own.
> When I fall short, I will acknowledge what I did and seek repair without demanding forgiveness.
> I vow to practice being kin through care for others and promises I can keep.

People may attend and try the practice without taking the vow. No signature, public declaration, ideological test, or record of pledges is required. Everyone who attends agrees to the meeting's conduct rules. If a promise cannot be kept, acknowledge it and discuss a realistic change with the person affected. They do not owe forgiveness or another chance.

## Six pillars

| Pillar | What it asks of us | An ordinary practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Knowledge | Stay curious and check what we think we know. | Ask a sincere question; verify a claim before repeating it. |
| Identity | Let people describe themselves and choose what to share. | Use the name someone offers; avoid asking them to speak for a whole group. |
| Narrative | Make room for the experiences behind a person's views. | Ask what shaped a view without demanding a personal story. |
| Voice | Give people a fair chance to speak, pass, and be heard. | Share the available time and offer a written response option. |
| Oneness | Recognize shared humanity while respecting real differences. | Cooperate on a useful task without requiring agreement on other matters. |
| Worth | Treat human dignity as something everyone already has. | Set a boundary without humiliation; challenge cruelty even within your own circle. |

These pillars guide practice. Participants do not need to memorize them. A personal account deserves a fair hearing; factual claims remain open to examination.

## The practice

The everyday invitation is **One conversation. One kept commitment.** These are related habits, not a requirement to make a new promise after each conversation. Ask someone willing what they would like you to understand. Listen, check your understanding, and offer advice only if invited. Passing, changing the topic, or stopping is allowed.

Listening can be enough. If an action is wanted, choose something useful that fits your capacity. Keeping an existing promise or respecting a requested boundary counts. Follow through and, when welcome, ask whether it helped. Existing relationships and ordinary conversations count. No one needs to seek out a political opponent or someone who has harmed them.

Proposed Kin Tables bring 8-12 adult participants, with a target of 12, together with two hosts. A modest shared task is optional and depends on need, permission, capacity, and participant interest. People affected by a proposed task help decide whether it would be useful. Photos, testimonials, and public proof are unnecessary.

The proposed 90-day pilot includes preparation on days 1-14, four gatherings on days 15-21, 35-42, 56-63, and 77-84, and review on days 85-90. The internal account follows day 90. Gatherings are roughly three weeks apart; preparation may take longer and no launch date is fixed. See the [host guide](host-guide.md) for the schedule and conditions to meet before opening a pilot.

## National purpose and local responsibility

The proposed movement is national in purpose and locally practiced. Start with a small adult pilot. Its findings can support a decision to repeat, revise, or stop. Any later expansion should follow useful local experiences, responsible hosting, and a further test led by people other than the founder. A small pilot cannot establish national effectiveness.

The following principles apply to the proposed organization and any future recognized local group:

- Welcome adults who agree to the conduct rules, without a party affiliation or citizenship test. Participation remains voluntary.
- Keep the organization's name, funds, meetings, and channels free of endorsements of candidates or political parties. Participants retain their own civic commitments. Public issues and injustice may be discussed.
- Apply conduct rules consistently. Equal dignity does not mean that every claim is true or every action causes equal harm.
- Make leadership responsibilities, financial decisions, and substantial rule changes visible to participants. Record reasons for decisions and allow criticism.
- Set review dates for leadership roles. Disclose conflicts of interest and use an uninvolved reviewer for complaints about a decision-maker.
- Give local hosts room to choose activities while preserving the vow's voluntary character, participant boundaries, and accountability rules.
- Establish a published recognition and complaint process before authorizing groups to present themselves as official chapters. No chapter or national staff is created by this document.

## Conduct and boundaries

Participants may criticize ideas, institutions, policies, and conduct, including forcefully. Anger, disagreement, or discomfort alone is not misconduct. Nobody must defend their right to participate as an equal or disclose personal experiences to justify a concern.

Threats, intimidation, discriminatory harassment, repeated personal humiliation, unwanted contact, and retaliation are grounds for intervention. Participants must respect requests to stop contact. Recruitment for parties, sales pitches, and fundraising are outside the pilot's meeting purpose.

People may pass, take a break, leave, or decline contact afterward. Ask before sharing contact details. Do not record, photograph, or quote another participant. Share your own learning without identifying other people or repeating their stories.

## Accountability and repair

Hosts interrupt specific misconduct and explain the behavior that needs to stop. Depending on severity, a response may be a clear boundary, a break, a condition for continuing, or removal. Serious conduct does not require a series of warnings before action.

Affected people can state what they need and choose whether to participate in any repair. They do not have to confront the other person, accept an apology, reconcile, or remain at the table. Repair may include changed behavior, correcting a false statement, replacing something damaged, or respecting a request for no contact.

A person whose conduct is questioned should receive a clear explanation and an opportunity to respond when that can happen without exposing others to further harm. An uninvolved reviewer should consider appeals. Immediate protective measures may remain in place during review. A concern alone is not a finding; a lack of proof is not a reason to retaliate against the person who raised it.

Before any gathering, hosts must identify a usable feedback route and a reviewer for concerns about hosts. The [host guide](host-guide.md) supplies the process and a blank notice to complete. The [evaluation plan](evaluation-plan.md) keeps learning separate from private incident records.

## Review and adoption

The founder should review this charter alongside the name and formation work before adopting a public version. A later governing body should record its adoption, publish changes, and review the charter after the first pilot. Adoption of movement commitments must be distinguished from legal formation and any authority to use the name.
